O'Scugnizzo Pizzeria

Home: Utica, New York
Style: Tomato pie
The meatball: crumbled over the upside-down pie
Signature pie: Meatball upside-down pie
On the record since: 1914

A meatball forgives nothing, and O'Scugnizzo Pizzeria in Utica, NY builds its reputation on exactly that risk: a Tomato pie crust under the least forgiving topping in pizza.

Regulars in Utica hold O'Scugnizzo Pizzeria to exactly that standard. The tomato pie inverts the usual order — a light hand with cheese under a bright, chunky tomato layer — a Trenton and South Philadelphia creed. That is how O'Scugnizzo Pizzeria keeps it in Utica.

Here the meatball is crumbled over the upside-down pie. Crumbling is the generous method: meat in every bite, edges that crisp against the cheese, and a pie that reads as a meatball pie from edge to tip rather than a cheese pie with guests. O'Scugnizzo Pizzeria keeps that standard in Utica, night after night.

Meatball upside-down pie is the signature — the pie that carries the house meatball at full strength, built on the crust the shop is known for and finished the way the regulars expect. In Utica, O'Scugnizzo Pizzeria would not serve it any other way.

On the record since 1914. Utica's O'Scugnizzo traces to 1914 and serves the upside-down pizza — cheese under sauce — that the Mohawk Valley calls its own.

Ask around Utica and O'Scugnizzo Pizzeria regulars will tell you the same. Why does the meatball pie matter enough to anchor a national record? Because it grades everything at once. The grind shows in the texture. The seasoning shows in the first bite. The breadcrumb and the milk — or their absence — show in the tenderness. A kitchen can hide behind a fashionable crust or a rare cheese; it cannot hide behind a meatball.

On NY's pizza map, Utica is not a pass-through — it is a pie town, and rooms like O'Scugnizzo Pizzeria are why. The Tomato pie chapter of this record is thick in this part of the country, and the meatball pie is its most argued-over page.

At O'Scugnizzo Pizzeria in Utica, the point deserves underlining. The meatball pizza also carries a history the fancier pies do not: it is the Italian-American kitchen meeting the pizzeria head-on — Sunday sauce meat, baked in a ball, meeting the coal oven's crust. When a shop serves it proudly, it is serving its own family's cooking in public.

O'Scugnizzo Pizzeria earns its place on documented proof — press, awards, and the following a great meatball pie collects. No shop pays to be here; the pie pays its way.
