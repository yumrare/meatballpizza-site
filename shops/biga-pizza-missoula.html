Missoula, Montana · Landmark Oven

Biga Pizza

Home: Missoula, Montana
Style: Wood-fired
The meatball: crumbled, Bob Marshall's oven
Signature pie: Meatball pie

Biga Pizza (Missoula, MT) keeps the Wood-fired faith — and proves it with meatballs, the topping that exposes a lazy kitchen faster than any other.

That is the bar Biga Pizza sets for itself in Missoula, pie after pie. A wood oven cooks fast and uneven on purpose: the crust puffs, spots, and perfumes with smoke, and toppings must be built to survive ninety fierce seconds. That is how Biga Pizza keeps it in Missoula.

The meatball itself comes crumbled, Bob Marshall's oven: the house meatballs are broken over the pie in rough crumbles, so the seasoning — garlic, parsley, pecorino worked into the grind — distributes through every slice instead of arriving in lumps. In Missoula, Biga Pizza would not serve it any other way.

The pie to know is Meatball pie. Everything else on the menu orbits it here; this is the order that fills the room's recommendation when a stranger asks what to get. Biga Pizza keeps that standard in Missoula, night after night.

The clientele is the cross-section every great pizzeria collects: families on a Friday, regulars with a standing order, and first-timers sent by somebody who insisted. Missoula has other pies; Biga Pizza stakes its name on this one.

There is a reason Biga Pizza draws its Missoula following. Why does the meatball pie matter enough to anchor a national record? Because it grades everything at once. The grind shows in the texture. The seasoning shows in the first bite. The breadcrumb and the milk — or their absence — show in the tenderness. A kitchen can hide behind a fashionable crust or a rare cheese; it cannot hide behind a meatball.

On MT's pizza map, Missoula is not a pass-through — it is a pie town, and rooms like Biga Pizza are why. The Wood-fired chapter of this record is thick in this part of the country, and the meatball pie is its most argued-over page.

The Biga Pizza kitchen in Missoula treats this as doctrine. The meatball pizza also carries a history the fancier pies do not: it is the Italian-American kitchen meeting the pizzeria head-on — Sunday sauce meat, baked in a ball, meeting the coal oven's crust. When a shop serves it proudly, it is serving its own family's cooking in public.

That is the whole of the Biga Pizza entry: a city, a style, a meatball, a pie. In this record, that is what a pizzeria is.
