Lombardi's

Home: New York, New York
Style: Coal-fired
The meatball: crumbled over a margherita base
Signature pie: The original margherita with meatballs
On the record since: 1905

A meatball forgives nothing, and Lombardi's in New York, NY builds its reputation on exactly that risk: a Coal-fired crust under the least forgiving topping in pizza.

Lombardi's works to that standard in New York — and asks to be judged by it. Coal heat is the old New York fire — hotter and drier than wood, blistering a crust in minutes and leaving the signature char that whole generations grew up on. It is the Lombardi's way in New York, and the regulars hold them to it.

The meatball itself comes crumbled over a margherita base: the house meatballs are broken over the pie in rough crumbles, so the seasoning — garlic, parsley, pecorino worked into the grind — distributes through every slice instead of arriving in lumps. Lombardi's keeps that standard in New York, night after night.

The signature is The original margherita with meatballs. On the menu it may sit among a dozen pies, but in this record it is the one that counts: the pie the kitchen's meatball was born for. New York has other pies; Lombardi's stakes its name on this one.

On the record since 1905. Gennaro Lombardi opened America's first licensed pizzeria on Spring Street in 1905; the coal-fired margherita lineage every New York pie descends from starts here.

At Lombardi's in New York, the point deserves underlining. Why does the meatball pie matter enough to anchor a national record? Because it grades everything at once. The grind shows in the texture. The seasoning shows in the first bite. The breadcrumb and the milk — or their absence — show in the tenderness. A kitchen can hide behind a fashionable crust or a rare cheese; it cannot hide behind a meatball.

On NY's pizza map, New York is not a pass-through — it is a pie town, and rooms like Lombardi's are why. The Coal-fired chapter of this record is thick in this part of the country, and the meatball pie is its most argued-over page.

The Lombardi's kitchen in New York treats this as doctrine. The meatball pizza also carries a history the fancier pies do not: it is the Italian-American kitchen meeting the pizzeria head-on — Sunday sauce meat, baked in a ball, meeting the coal oven's crust. When a shop serves it proudly, it is serving its own family's cooking in public.

Lombardi's earns its place on documented proof — press, awards, and the following a great meatball pie collects. No shop pays to be here; the pie pays its way.
