Cane Rosso

Home: Dallas, Texas
Style: Neapolitan
The meatball: crumbled, Jay Jerrier's ovens
Signature pie: Meatball pie

Cane Rosso works in the Neapolitan tradition in Dallas, TX, and its meatball pie is the house examination: grind, seasoning, crumb, and crust all graded at once.

At Cane Rosso in Dallas, that standard is not theory — it is the nightly measure. The Neapolitan school is a discipline: a soft, leopard-spotted crust from a fierce oven, San Marzano-style tomato, and restraint everywhere. Meatballs here are used sparingly, the way Naples would insist. It is the Cane Rosso way in Dallas, and the regulars hold them to it.

The meatball itself comes crumbled, Jay Jerrier's ovens: the house meatballs are broken over the pie in rough crumbles, so the seasoning — garlic, parsley, pecorino worked into the grind — distributes through every slice instead of arriving in lumps. Cane Rosso keeps that standard in Dallas, night after night.

The signature is Meatball pie. On the menu it may sit among a dozen pies, but in this record it is the one that counts: the pie the kitchen's meatball was born for. Cane Rosso keeps that standard in Dallas, night after night.

The room runs the way good pizzerias do: a counter, an oven you can watch, boxes stacked for the takeout trade, and a rhythm that peaks at dinner and never quite empties. Cane Rosso keeps that standard in Dallas, night after night.

There is a reason Cane Rosso draws its Dallas following. Why does the meatball pie matter enough to anchor a national record? Because it grades everything at once. The grind shows in the texture. The seasoning shows in the first bite. The breadcrumb and the milk — or their absence — show in the tenderness. A kitchen can hide behind a fashionable crust or a rare cheese; it cannot hide behind a meatball.

On TX's pizza map, Dallas is not a pass-through — it is a pie town, and rooms like Cane Rosso are why. The Neapolitan chapter of this record is thick in this part of the country, and the meatball pie is its most argued-over page.

The meatball pizza also carries a history the fancier pies do not: it is the Italian-American kitchen meeting the pizzeria head-on — Sunday sauce meat, baked in a ball, meeting the coal oven's crust. When a shop serves it proudly, it is serving its own family's cooking in public.
