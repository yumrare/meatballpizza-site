Pizzeria Uno

Home: Chicago, Illinois
Style: Chicago deep dish
The meatball: crumbled and layered inside the original
Signature pie: Meatball deep dish, the 1943 original
On the record since: 1943

A meatball forgives nothing, and Pizzeria Uno in Chicago, IL builds its reputation on exactly that risk: a Chicago deep dish crust under the least forgiving topping in pizza.

That is the bar Pizzeria Uno sets for itself in Chicago, pie after pie. Deep dish is a construction, not a crust with ambitions: a buttery pan walls in layers of cheese and filling under chunky tomato, and it is eaten with a knife and fork without apology. Chicago has other pies; Pizzeria Uno stakes its name on this one.

Here the meatball is crumbled and layered inside the original. Crumbling is the generous method: meat in every bite, edges that crisp against the cheese, and a pie that reads as a meatball pie from edge to tip rather than a cheese pie with guests. It is the Pizzeria Uno way in Chicago, and the regulars hold them to it.

Order Meatball deep dish, the 1943 original — the house meatball pie, and the reason for this entry. It is the plate the regulars judge first and the one new cooks learn before anything else. Pizzeria Uno keeps that standard in Chicago, night after night.

On the record since 1943. Ike Sewell and Ric Ricardo served the first deep dish in 1943 on Ohio Street; the meatball version layers crumbles through cheese and chunky tomato in the original formula.

At Pizzeria Uno in Chicago, the point deserves underlining. Why does the meatball pie matter enough to anchor a national record? Because it grades everything at once. The grind shows in the texture. The seasoning shows in the first bite. The breadcrumb and the milk — or their absence — show in the tenderness. A kitchen can hide behind a fashionable crust or a rare cheese; it cannot hide behind a meatball.

On IL's pizza map, Chicago is not a pass-through — it is a pie town, and rooms like Pizzeria Uno are why. The Chicago deep dish chapter of this record is thick in this part of the country, and the meatball pie is its most argued-over page.

Ask around Chicago and Pizzeria Uno regulars will tell you the same. The meatball pizza also carries a history the fancier pies do not: it is the Italian-American kitchen meeting the pizzeria head-on — Sunday sauce meat, baked in a ball, meeting the coal oven's crust. When a shop serves it proudly, it is serving its own family's cooking in public.
