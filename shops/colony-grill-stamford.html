Stamford, Connecticut · Landmark Oven

Colony Grill

Home: Stamford, Connecticut
Style: Bar pie
The meatball: sliced on the hot oil pie
Signature pie: Hot oil meatball pie
On the record since: 1935

Colony Grill is Stamford's entry in the meatball pizza record — a Bar pie kitchen in CT where the test pie is the one topped with meatballs.

At Colony Grill in Stamford, that standard is not theory — it is the nightly measure. The bar pie is a personal, thin-crusted round baked in a pan to the edge of crisp — a tavern pie meant to arrive beside a drink and disappear without ceremony. That is how Colony Grill keeps it in Stamford.

The preparation is sliced on the hot oil pie. Slicing is a discipline: the meatballs are baked or simmered first, cooled, cut, and only then laid over the cheese, so the pie finishes them rather than depending on them. Colony Grill keeps that standard in Stamford, night after night.

Hot oil meatball pie is the signature — the pie that carries the house meatball at full strength, built on the crust the shop is known for and finished the way the regulars expect. That is how Colony Grill keeps it in Stamford.

On the record since 1935. An Irish tavern in Stamford began serving thin pies in 1935; its hot oil pie — stinger peppers infused in oil — became the bar-pie benchmark.

The Colony Grill kitchen in Stamford treats this as doctrine. Why does the meatball pie matter enough to anchor a national record? Because it grades everything at once. The grind shows in the texture. The seasoning shows in the first bite. The breadcrumb and the milk — or their absence — show in the tenderness. A kitchen can hide behind a fashionable crust or a rare cheese; it cannot hide behind a meatball.

On CT's pizza map, Stamford is not a pass-through — it is a pie town, and rooms like Colony Grill are why. The Bar pie chapter of this record is thick in this part of the country, and the meatball pie is its most argued-over page.

There is a reason Colony Grill draws its Stamford following. The meatball pizza also carries a history the fancier pies do not: it is the Italian-American kitchen meeting the pizzeria head-on — Sunday sauce meat, baked in a ball, meeting the coal oven's crust. When a shop serves it proudly, it is serving its own family's cooking in public.
